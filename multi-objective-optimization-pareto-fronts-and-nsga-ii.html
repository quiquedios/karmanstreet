Multi-objective optimization, Pareto fronts and NSGA-II

Theory · sources: thesis

Expected Improvement is a single-objective criterion: it ranks designs by improvement over one best-so-far value. Most real design problems are not like that. The front-wing work pursues two competing objectives at once — maximizing downforce in the cornering mode while minimizing drag in the straight-line mode — so "improvement" can no longer be a single number.

Hypervolume

What is needed instead is a measure of how good the whole set of trade-off designs is. That measure is the hypervolume, easiest to picture on the CD–CL plot itself. Take the designs evaluated so far and keep only the non-dominated ones — the best compromises. Together with a reference point fixed deliberately worse than any design of interest, these solutions enclose a region of the plot: the area of all outcomes that the current designs already beat.

That area is the hypervolume, and it condenses the quality of the whole front into a single number. The larger the area, the better and the more spread-out the designs are.

The acquisition function used is built directly on this: the q-Expected Hypervolume Improvement, which scores a candidate batch of q designs by how much they would enlarge the hypervolume. The bracket is simply the new area minus the current area, and maximising it selects the batch that expands the dominated region most — which is precisely what pushes the front toward higher downforce and lower drag at once. Choosing q designs together as a batch also suits the workflow, since several CFD cases can be prepared and run together between successive re-fits.

Genetic algorithms

Once the active-learning loop concludes and the surrogates are frozen, the final optimum must be extracted from them. Since evaluating a fitted GP is cheap, this last step is performed not with further Bayesian iterations but with a genetic algorithm run directly over the surrogate means.

A genetic algorithm is a population-based, gradient-free optimizer inspired by natural selection: a population of candidate designs is repeatedly recombined through crossover and perturbed through mutation, and the fittest members are retained each generation, so the population evolves toward better solutions. Its gradient-free nature suits the surrogate, which does not need to be differentiated, and its population naturally explores many designs in parallel.

NSGA-II and the Pareto front

The specific algorithm used is NSGA-II, the standard genetic algorithm for multi-objective problems. Rather than optimizing a single blended objective, it ranks the population by non-domination and preserves solution diversity through a crowding measure. A single run therefore returns not one design but a whole spread of mutually optimal trade-offs.

Two mechanisms make this work. The first is non-dominated sorting. A design a dominates a design b if it is at least as good in both objectives and strictly better in one. The population is sorted into successive fronts by this relation: the first front is the set of designs dominated by none, the second those dominated only by members of the first, and so on. Selection then favors designs in the earlier fronts.

The second is the crowding measure, a metric of how isolated each design is from its neighbours, used to favor spread-out designs over clustered ones so the returned front covers the trade-off rather than bunching in one region.

Every point on the front is an optimal compromise, so the algorithm does not choose for you. In the thesis, the selection among them was made per circuit, using the fraction of each lap spent with DRS open as the weighting between the two modes.
