Discretisation and mesh quality

Foundations

A solver does not manipulate continuous fields; it manipulates numbers stored at discrete locations. Turning the governing equations into something a computer can advance is discretisation, and the quality of that step sets a ceiling on the accuracy of everything above it. No turbulence model rescues a solution that its own numerics have already corrupted.

The finite-volume method

The dominant approach in engineering CFD is the finite-volume method. The domain is divided into control volumes, and the conservation laws of Part I are enforced over each one by balancing the fluxes crossing its faces. Because it is built on flux balances rather than on differential operators, the transported quantities are conserved exactly and by construction — which is why it is trusted for aerodynamics, where the output of interest is an integrated force.

Getting from cell-centred values to the face fluxes requires interpolation, and that choice is where accuracy is won or lost. First-order schemes are stable but add numerical diffusion that smears the very gradients being resolved; higher-order schemes are sharper but more prone to oscillation. The supersonic-wedge project on this site is a clean demonstration: the same flow, captured sharply or smeared into a thick band, depending only on the scheme.

Mesh quality

Two meshes with the same cell count can give very different answers. Quality is described by a few standard metrics: skewness (how distorted a cell is from its ideal shape), aspect ratio (the ratio of its longest to shortest dimension, acceptable when aligned with the flow and harmful when not), and non-orthogonality (the angle between the line joining two cell centres and the face they share, which feeds error into every gradient). A good mesh keeps these within bounds, refines where gradients are steep — walls, leading edges, wakes, shear layers — and stays coarse where the flow is uniform.
