Finite-difference schemes, Von Neumann stability and the Courant number

Theory · sources: thesis

Solving a partial differential equation numerically means replacing derivatives with differences on a grid. The scheme chosen to do that governs not only the accuracy of the answer but whether there is an answer at all — an unstable scheme will not converge to a wrong result, it will diverge.

The testbed

The 1D linear advection equation is the standard vehicle for this, simple enough to have an exact solution and rich enough to expose everything that can go wrong. Three discretizations were implemented in Python: first-order backward (upwind), second-order central, and second-order backward.

Von Neumann analysis

Stability was assessed with Von Neumann stability analysis, decomposing the solution into Fourier modes and deriving the amplification factor of each scheme — the complex factor by which a mode is multiplied every time step. If its magnitude exceeds one for any mode, that mode grows without bound and the scheme is unstable.

The analysis shows that the second-order central differencing scheme is inherently unstable for explicit time integration, regardless of how small the time step is made. Upwind-biased schemes are stable within a limit, but pay for it with numerical diffusion that smears sharp features.

The governing stability parameter is the Courant number, which links the physical advection speed to the grid resolution and the time step:

C = a Δt / Δx

It has a direct physical reading: it is how far information travels in one time step, measured in cells. Exceed the scheme's limit and the numerical domain of dependence no longer contains the physical one.

Results and the order barrier

Time-step and mesh refinement studies identified the conditions for convergence (dt = 10⁻⁴, N = 200) and compared the schemes on oscillatory behaviour and error propagation.

The results confirm the theoretical expectations, including Godunov's order barrier theorem: a linear, monotone scheme cannot exceed first-order accuracy. That is the fundamental tension the whole exercise illustrates — stability and accuracy pull against each other in linear schemes, which is precisely why practical solvers reach for nonlinear flux limiters to escape the barrier.
