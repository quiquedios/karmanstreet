Lift via the Newtonian / momentum view

Theory · sources: thesis · A

The mechanism by which lift is generated is widely misunderstood. It is often stated that air particles divided at the leading edge reach the trailing edge at the same time, leading to a higher velocity on the longest side of the airfoil, which results in a pressure imbalance — the net force called lift. This explanation, known as Equal Transit Theory, is taught even at rather advanced levels and has been proven incorrect. The suction side does accelerate the airflow, but the air paths do not recombine at the trailing edge at the same time. And even though the airfoil does produce a velocity and a pressure difference, the explanation is missing other physical phenomena involved, as the velocity difference alone does not account for the totality of the lift observed.

In reality, lift generation is a combination of mechanisms and can be explained by different observations of the same phenomenon. This is perhaps the most important and non-intuitive point to understand: there is not a Newtonian component and a Bernoulli component that add up to a total lift — they are both different observations of the same thing.

The Newtonian interpretation

The Newtonian interpretation is sometimes understood as a momentum exchange in which air molecules collide with the wing, producing an action-reaction pair. The idea of an action-reaction pair is not wrong per se, but thinking that the air is deflected exclusively through molecular collisions misses information about the flow. It raises the question of how the flow is deflected downwards in a continuous fluid.

The tendency of the flow to follow the curvature of the airfoil is governed by pressure gradients in the outer flow and by boundary-layer dynamics near the wall. As the flow moves along a curved surface, a pressure gradient normal to the streamline is required to provide the centripetal acceleration necessary to bend the flow:

∂p/∂n = ρ V² / R

where R is the local radius of curvature of the streamlines. Without such a normal pressure gradient, the streamlines simply could not bend around the airfoil.

Near the wall, viscosity gives rise to a boundary layer within which the flow has reduced momentum. Whether the flow stays attached therefore depends on the streamwise pressure gradient. Neglecting viscosity and gravity in the outer airflow, the momentum equation reduces to Dv/Dt = −∇p/ρ, which shows that favorable pressure gradients accelerate the flow whereas adverse pressure gradients decelerate it.

Now, because of mass conservation, a fluid arbitrarily close to a solid, non-porous surface must have zero perpendicular velocity, as no mass moves in or out. The velocity must therefore be completely tangent, which leaves three possible scenarios:

  1. The fluid travels tangent to the surface.
  2. The fluid is stationary, which occurs at stagnation points.
  3. The flow is reversed, resulting in flow separation.

Under a favorable pressure gradient the boundary layer remains attached and the flow follows the surface curvature. Conversely, sufficiently strong adverse pressure gradients lead first to stagnation and then to separation. Flow attachment is therefore not the result of a distinct effect, but of the balance between pressure gradients and viscous effects within the boundary layer.

Momentum exchange through Navier–Stokes

At this point it is understood how a cambered wing can orient the flow downwards, but the process by which momentum is exchanged still needs addressing. Taking the integral form of the momentum equation, with negligible body forces and a steady problem, and noting that there is no flux across the solid wing, the force exerted by the wing on the flow is:

FW→F = ∫S −p n dS + ∫S τ · n dS

This states that the force must be congruent with the boundary conditions of the flow, and that it must be the sum of a pressure and a (typically smaller) viscous contribution at the surface of the wing. It explains why, looking at the wake, the horizontal velocity is lower and there is a downward component — showing drag and lift respectively.

Once it is understood how the wing transfers momentum to the air, the rest follows. In a scenario where both air and wing move horizontally, a direct interpretation of Newton's third law explains that if air is being deflected downwards by some force, an equal and opposite reaction must be exerted on the wing. That reaction is lift.

The Bernoulli interpretation, and circulation

There is also a Bernoulli interpretation of lift, which is just as correct. The widely repeated version is right in relating the pressure imbalance to different velocities over the upper and lower surfaces, but it usually ignores why the velocities are different, which is the fundamental part.

Neglecting the height term, since a fluid as light as air over the height variation of an airfoil causes insignificant pressure variation, Bernoulli's theorem reads:

p + ½ ρ v² = constant

The direct interpretation is that regions of higher velocity have lower pressure, and vice versa. This is why the stagnant leading edge, where incoming flow has been slowed down, has a much higher pressure than the freestream. What remains is to explain why the flow is faster over the upper surface, and that is the role of circulation and the Kutta condition.

According to circulation theory, the flow around an airfoil develops a net circulation Γ when the airfoil is cambered, at incidence, or both. This circulation arises as a consequence of the Kutta condition, which enforces a smooth flow departure at the trailing edge and selects a unique, physically realizable solution among the infinite inviscid possibilities. Adding that circulation to the incoming uniform freestream produces a velocity field in which the upper surface accelerates while the lower surface slows down — precisely the asymmetric distribution needed for the pressure difference predicted by Bernoulli.

It is also worth recording that the establishment of circulation is accompanied by the shedding of a starting vortex from the trailing edge, ensuring conservation of total circulation in accordance with Kelvin's circulation theorem.

Five interdependent components

A good closing point is the one given by Doug McLean, who explains that there are five components behind lift generation:

  1. A deflection of free flow
  2. An increase in air velocity
  3. A low-pressure area
  4. A high-pressure area
  5. A cause-effect relation by which neither of the previous four exists without the others

This is why lift generation is best understood as a combination of velocity changes, pressure imbalances and flow deflection, all of which are interdependent.
