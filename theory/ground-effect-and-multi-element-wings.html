Ground effect and multi-element wings

Theory · Thesis §2.1.1

Once the benefits of downforce are understood, the question becomes how to generate it. The most straightforward approach is the installation of inverted wings: the aerodynamic mechanisms are identical to those of an aircraft wing, and by inverting them lift is turned into downforce. Inverted wings are typical in both closed-wheelers and open-wheelers, although the former are usually limited to rear-mounted wings while the latter allow front wings, rear wings and even side wings.

Ground effect

A second, less obvious approach is the exploitation of ground effect. When a downforce-generating surface is placed in close proximity to the ground, the flow accelerated underneath it is further constrained by the road, producing a stronger suction peak and consequently more downforce.

As the ride height is progressively reduced, downforce increases until a critical height-to-chord ratio is reached, beyond which the boundary layer underneath can no longer withstand the adverse pressure gradient and separates, abruptly losing load. Because the front wing is the lowest and foremost aerodynamic surface of the car, it operates permanently in this regime, and ground effect is therefore inseparable from its design.

Multi-element wings

Downforce is rarely produced by a single surface working in isolation. Modern front wings are multi-element, stacking several airfoil profiles separated by small gaps. Each slot re-energises the boundary layer of the element downstream, allowing the assembly to reach far higher effective angles of attack — and therefore far higher downforce — before stalling than a single element ever could. It is the same slotted-flap principle used on aircraft high-lift devices, applied here in inverted form.

The four steps

A real question the author was asked when interviewing for an aerodynamics position in a motorsport team: "You get a bounding box in freestream air. How do you maximize the downforce while limiting drag?" The answer:

  1. Inverted wing.
  2. Close to the ground for ground effect.
  3. Multi-element, to increase the effective angle of attack without flow separation.
  4. Endplates, to reduce the induced drag.

This procedure is not exclusive to front wings; it is standard for other devices such as the rear wing, with the difference that ground effect is considerably weaker there since it sits higher. An additional consideration is that the wing mounts should be placed on the pressure side, as the suction side is more delicate and prone to detachment. For a front wing that is the natural location, but for a rear wing it leads to the less obvious arrangements known as goose-necks or swan-necks — and sometimes the mounts are moved behind the trailing edge entirely so as not to disturb the incoming flow.
