Surrogate models and Gaussian processes

Theory · from TFM §2.1

Each evaluation of a design — in the thesis, a pair of CFD cases, one for CL in the cornering mode and one for CD in the straight-line mode — is far too costly to call repeatedly inside an optimizer. A surrogate model avoids this: it is a cheap-to-evaluate function fitted to the available data that approximates the true input-output relationship and replaces the simulation during the search. The optimizer can then call the surrogate thousands of times at negligible cost, while the expensive CFD evaluations are reserved exclusively for training it.

Gaussian processes

The surrogate adopted is a Gaussian Process, also known as Kriging. Unlike a model that returns a single predicted value, a GP returns at every unseen input a full predictive distribution with a mean µ(x) and a variance σ²(x):

$$\mu(x_*) = k_*^{\top} K^{-1} y, \qquad \sigma^2(x_*) = k(x_*,x_*) - k_*^{\top} K^{-1} k_*$$

Despite their appearance, these carry a simple meaning. The predicted mean is nothing more than a weighted average of the observed responses, where the weights give most influence to the training points whose inputs are closest to x*. The prediction is therefore dominated by nearby data and barely affected by distant data, exactly the behaviour expected of a smooth interpolator.

The variance has an equally intuitive reading: it starts from the prior uncertainty and subtracts a term representing how much the training data constrains the value at x*. When x* lies close to observed points that subtracted term is large and the variance collapses toward zero; when x* lies far from all data it vanishes and the variance returns to its full prior value. This is precisely why the model is confident near the data and uncertain in the gaps.

The kernel, and what it reveals

The kernel chosen is a squared-exponential with Automatic Relevance Determination, meaning a separate length-scale is fitted for each design variable rather than a single shared one. The kernel is largest when two designs coincide and decays as they move apart, expressing the idea that similar designs give similar outputs. The length-scales set how fast that decay happens along each axis independently: a large ℓd means the variable must change significantly before the correlation drops, whereas a small ℓd makes it fall off sharply.

This per-dimension behaviour is what gives ARD its usefulness. Once fitted, the length-scales can be read directly as a measure of influence — a large ℓd marks a variable the output is insensitive to, a small one a variable of strong influence. The fitted kernel therefore delivers a data-driven sensitivity ranking of the design variables as a natural by-product of training, which partially compensates for the absence of a dedicated sensitivity analysis before sampling.

A noise term is also fitted and added to the diagonal, which is what lets the GP pass near the CFD points rather than interpolating each one exactly, tolerating their residual numerical scatter.
