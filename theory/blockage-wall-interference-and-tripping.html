Blockage, wall interference and tripping

Theory · from TFM §2.1

A wind tunnel provides the experimental counterpart to CFD, measuring the aerodynamic forces on a physical model placed in a controlled airstream. Its role is to provide an independent point of validation for numerical results — which only works if the model flow genuinely represents the full-scale flow.

Dynamic similarity

The theoretical basis of all wind-tunnel testing is dynamic similarity: a scaled model reproduces the full-scale flow faithfully only if the relevant non-dimensional groups are matched between the two. For incompressible aerodynamics the governing group is the Reynolds number:

Re = ρ U c / µ = U c / ν

where c is a reference length, in this case the effective wing chord. Matching Reynolds number for the same fluid at incompressible regime requires Umodel cmodel = Ureal creal. Since a scaled model has a smaller chord, the tunnel speed would have to exceed the real speed to compensate — which, for the very high speeds of a racing car, is unattainable in a small tunnel.

Full dynamic similarity is typically only attainable for F1 teams with tremendously large and fast wind tunnels, and even their models are usually at 60% scale. In the thesis work, a 32.4% scale model in a tunnel reaching 50 m/s reproduces real speeds close to 60 km/h, which in F1 corresponds to a slow corner. Because the Reynolds number differs from the modelled speed, correlation is not exactly held — a compromise forced by tight constraints, and one worth stating openly rather than hiding.

Blockage and wall proximity

Because the model sits inside a bounded test section rather than in free air, its measurements are subject to systematic effects that must be kept small by sizing the model appropriately. Two rules of thumb govern this:

  • Blockage ratio. The model obstructs part of the tunnel cross-section, artificially accelerating the flow around it and inflating the measured forces. The effect is limited by keeping the model frontal area to a small fraction of the test-section area — conventionally around 5 to 10% — beyond which correction factors become large and unreliable.
  • Wall proximity. Proximity to the tunnel walls distorts the flow field a lifting body would have in free air, an effect known as wall interference. It is contained by keeping sufficient distance between model and walls, in practice by limiting the model span to a fraction of the tunnel width.

Standard correction procedures exist for the residual blockage and interference that remain once the model is sensibly sized.

Tripping the boundary layer

A further consideration is specific to reduced scale. At low Reynolds number a significant portion of the boundary layer may remain laminar, whereas at full scale it would be turbulent almost everywhere. This mismatch matters because a laminar boundary layer separates far more readily than a turbulent one and would misrepresent the aerodynamics — and moreover the CFD against which the model is compared assumes a fully turbulent boundary layer.

To reconcile the two, the boundary layer on the model is deliberately tripped: transition to turbulence is forced at a fixed location by a small surface disturbance, in this case tape near the leading edge, so that model, full-scale wing and simulation all share a turbulent boundary layer. Tripping does not recover the missing Reynolds number, but it ensures the flow regime is representative, which is what makes the comparison meaningful.
