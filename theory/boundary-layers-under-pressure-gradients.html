Boundary layers under pressure gradients

Theory · Project 50

A boundary layer does not develop in isolation; the pressure imposed by the outer flow shapes it profoundly. Whether that pressure is falling or rising along the surface makes the difference between attached flow and separation.

Under a favorable pressure gradient (pressure falling downstream) the boundary layer is thinned and energised, and stays firmly attached. Under an adverse pressure gradient (pressure rising) it thickens, the near-wall flow is decelerated, and if the gradient is strong enough the flow reverses and separates.

Simulating a flat-plate boundary layer under favorable, zero and adverse gradients with a marching finite-difference scheme reproduces these trends — thickening under zero gradient, thinning under a favorable one — and reveals a hard limit: under a strong adverse gradient the parabolic boundary-layer formulation itself breaks down at separation, signalling the need for a full elliptic treatment. It is a compact lesson in why separation is where simple models stop working.
