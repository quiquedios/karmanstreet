Vortex shedding and the Kármán street

Theory · Project 28

Flow past a bluff body is one of the richest problems in fluid dynamics, and the origin of this site's name. What happens behind a circular cylinder depends almost entirely on the Reynolds number, and the progression through the regimes is worth following in order.

From steady wake to unsteady shedding

At low Reynolds numbers the flow is steady and symmetric. In the CFD study covering Re = 1–800, the steady-state solutions for Re ≤ 40 show the wake progressively thinning and the upstream stagnation region shrinking as convective effects come to dominate over viscous ones. The boundary layer separation angle moves forward over this range, decreasing from around 180° at very low Re to roughly 130° at Re = 40, indicating earlier flow detachment.

Past a critical value the symmetric wake becomes unstable and the cylinder begins shedding vortices alternately from each side. These roll downstream in the regular, staggered pattern known as the von Kármán vortex street. For Re ≥ 100 the simulation has to be run transient to capture it at all.

The shedding frequency

Extracting oscillation frequencies from velocity probes shows a strong correlation between Reynolds number and shedding frequency, climbing from about 2 Hz at Re = 100 to 25 Hz at Re = 800. Non-dimensionalising that frequency gives the Strouhal number:

St = f D / U

which came out in the range St ≈ 0.15–0.23, consistent with empirical data and showing the characteristic weak dependence on Reynolds number at the higher regimes. That near-constancy is what makes the Strouhal number useful: it lets the shedding frequency of a body be predicted from its size and the flow speed alone.

Measuring it experimentally

The same phenomenon can be caught directly with hot-wire anemometry. Sampling the wake of a 32 mm cylinder at high frequency and computing power spectral densities gives clean shedding peaks and their dependence on freestream velocity, with turbulence intensities of 27–32% in the wake. A short-time Fourier transform confirms the shedding frequency is stable in time and agrees with the Strouhal-based prediction.

Vortex shedding is not only an academic curiosity. It drives fatigue loads on structures, and in the air-brake project the vortex-induced vibration it causes had to be accounted for when sizing the supports.
