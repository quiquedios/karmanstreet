Divergence of a wing section and the divergence speed

Theory · sources: thesis

Aeroelasticity is what happens when a structure is flexible enough that aerodynamic loads and structural deformation feed back into one another. Divergence is the static instability at the end of that feedback loop: a twist increases the aerodynamic moment, which increases the twist, and past a critical speed the loop runs away.

Deriving the divergence speed

The model is a rigid wing section supported on an elastic torsional spring. Coupling the aerodynamic lift to the structural stiffness through moment equilibrium — taking moments about the elastic axis, with the lift acting at the aerodynamic centre — gives a closed-form expression for the divergence dynamic pressure and the corresponding critical velocity.

For the baseline geometric and structural conditions studied, that comes out at a divergence speed of 49.96 m/s. The number itself matters less than what it depends on.

What actually drives stability

A parametric design study identifies the key drivers: wing surface area, chord length, wingspan, and — the important one — the relative position of the aerodynamic centre and the elastic axis. The separation between those two points is the moment arm of the whole feedback loop, so moving the elastic axis forward or aft changes the divergence speed more effectively than almost anything else.

Quantifying those sensitivities made it possible to propose design modifications meeting a safety-constrained requirement of being divergence-free up to 69 m/s, achieved through geometric redesign and structural reconfiguration: reducing span, modifying the chord and relocating the elastic axis.

Divergence as a singularity

Extending the model to include non-zero pitching moment effects gives the nonlinear relationship between elastic twist and dynamic pressure, and makes the nature of the instability explicit: the divergence condition appears as a singularity in the system response. The twist does not grow gradually to failure — the denominator of the response goes to zero, and the predicted deflection becomes unbounded at a finite speed.
