How lift is generated

Theory · Thesis §2.1.1 · Project 27

A wing generates lift by turning the flow. If the air leaving the trailing edge is deflected downward, then by Newton's third law the air pushes the wing the opposite way — upward. This momentum argument and the pressure argument (lower pressure on the suction side, higher on the pressure side) are two views of the same event, not competing explanations.

Angle of attack and camber

Two things make a wing turn flow: its angle of attack (how much it is tilted into the stream) and its camber (how curved the section is). A flat plate at incidence produces lift perfectly well, which is the quickest way to see that the popular "the air travels farther over the top so it goes faster" story is wrong — a flat plate has equal path lengths and still lifts.

Bernoulli, the Kutta condition and circulation

Bernoulli's theorem links the faster flow over the suction side to its lower pressure, but it does not by itself say why the flow speeds up. That comes from circulation: the amount of net swirl bound to the aerofoil. The Kutta condition — the physical requirement that flow leaves the sharp trailing edge smoothly — selects exactly the circulation that makes the rear stagnation point sit at the trailing edge, and that circulation is what sets the lift. Kelvin's circulation theorem then explains where the equal-and-opposite starting vortex goes when a wing begins to move.
