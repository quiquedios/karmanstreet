Design of Experiments and Latin Hypercube Sampling

Theory · from TFM §2.1

Design of Experiments is the discipline of choosing where in the design space to place expensive evaluations. When the objective is to characterize a response over the whole domain — rather than to hill-climb from a starting point — a space-filling design is what is required.

Latin Hypercube Sampling

Latin Hypercube Sampling is the canonical choice. It partitions each of the n input dimensions into equal-probability intervals and draws exactly one sample per interval per dimension, guaranteeing that every variable is uniformly covered while the joint samples spread evenly across the space. It is the standard way to generate the initial training set that seeds a surrogate model.

Concretely, the construction proceeds one dimension at a time. For a single design variable, its normalised range [0,1] is divided into N equal sub-intervals. Within each, a single point is drawn at random, so there is exactly one candidate value per interval. These N values are then shuffled into random order and handed out to the N samples, one each. Repeating this independently for every dimension, and pairing the k-th value of each dimension to form sample k, yields the full design.

$$x_k^{(j)} = \frac{\pi_j(k) - 1 + U_k^{(j)}}{N}$$

where πj(k) is the interval assigned to sample k in dimension j — the outcome of the shuffle — and Uk(j) is an independent uniform random number that offsets the point within its interval so it does not sit exactly on the edge. Because the shuffle is drawn independently for each dimension, every interval of every variable is occupied exactly once while the pairing across dimensions is randomised, which is what produces the characteristic even spread.

Why not the alternatives

The advantage is best seen against two alternatives. A full-factorial grid, placing samples at every combination of intervals, requires Nn points and is hopeless in high dimension — for the 26 variables of the thesis work, even two levels per dimension would demand 2²⁶, over sixty million evaluations.

Pure random sampling uses an affordable number of points but tends to leave gaps in some regions while clustering in others, and in particular does not guarantee that any single variable is evenly covered.

LHS keeps the affordable sample count of random sampling while enforcing, by construction, the per-dimension uniformity that random sampling lacks. The projection of the design onto any single axis is always perfectly stratified, regardless of N or n, which is what makes it scalable while remaining affordable.

One limitation remains: the stratification guarantee applies to the one-dimensional projections, but a particular random pairing of intervals can still leave the joint space unevenly filled, with points aligning along diagonals or leaving multidimensional voids. More elaborate variants address this by generating many candidate hypercubes and retaining the one that best spreads the points.
