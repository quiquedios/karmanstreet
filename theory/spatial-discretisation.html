Spatial discretisation

Theory · from TFM §2.1

A solver cannot work with continuous fields. Spatial discretisation is the process of dividing the flow domain into a finite set of cells and rewriting the governing equations as algebraic relationships between values stored at discrete locations.

The finite-volume approach

The dominant approach in engineering CFD is the finite-volume method. The domain is divided into control volumes, and conservation of mass and momentum is enforced over each one by balancing the fluxes crossing its faces. Because the method is built directly on flux balances rather than on differential operators, the transported quantities are conserved by construction — which is the main reason it is trusted for aerodynamic work where integrated forces are the output of interest.

Getting from cell-centred values to the face fluxes that the balance requires means interpolating, and the choice of interpolation scheme is where accuracy is won or lost. First-order schemes are stable and robust but introduce substantial numerical diffusion, which smears exactly the gradients being resolved. Second-order schemes are sharper but more prone to oscillation near discontinuities.

Why this shows up in every result

This is not an abstract concern. In the supersonic wedge study the whole point was watching first versus second-order discretisation change how sharply an oblique shock was captured, with the lower-order schemes visibly thickening it through numerical diffusion. The same mechanism operates more subtly on a wing, where an over-diffusive scheme will misplace the separation point and therefore the pressure drag.

Together with the mesh itself — cell type, density, and where the refinement is placed — the discretisation scheme sets the fidelity ceiling of the entire simulation. No turbulence model compensates for a solution that has been smeared by its own numerics.
