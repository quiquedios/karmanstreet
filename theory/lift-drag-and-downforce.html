Lift, drag and downforce

Theory · Thesis §2.1.1

When a wing moves through air it feels a single net aerodynamic force. It is convenient to split that force into two components defined relative to the oncoming flow: lift, perpendicular to the freestream, and drag, parallel to it. The split is a choice of axes, not two separate physical effects — the air only ever exerts one resultant force, made up of pressure acting normal to the surface and shear acting along it.

Why downforce is just negative lift

In aircraft we want lift to point up. In motorsport we want the opposite: a wing turned upside-down pushes the car into the road, improving grip. That downward force is downforce — physically identical to lift, only with the sign flipped. Everything that governs lift (angle of attack, camber, circulation) governs downforce in exactly the same way.

Keeping the decomposition clear matters because the two components trade against each other. Adding downforce almost always adds drag, and the useful figure of merit is usually the ratio of the two, the aerodynamic efficiency L/D. Much of wing design is a negotiation over that ratio under whatever constraints apply.
