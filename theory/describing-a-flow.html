Describing a flow

Foundations

Once the fluid is characterised, the flow through it needs a vocabulary. A handful of distinctions, made early, determine which equations apply and which can be discarded.

Streamlines and stream tubes

A streamline is a curve everywhere tangent to the local velocity — the path a massless tracer would trace at one instant. Bundle a set of them and you get a stream tube, whose walls no flow crosses by construction. This is the mental picture behind every control-volume argument that follows, because a stream tube turns a messy flow field into something with a clear inside and outside.

Steady versus unsteady, and dimensionality

A flow is steady if the field at any fixed point does not change with time. Steadiness is a property of the reference frame, not of the flow in the abstract: the flow over a car is unsteady to a bystander but steady in the frame moving with the car, which is exactly why wind-tunnel and CFD analyses are set in that frame. Independently, a flow may be treated as one, two or three-dimensional depending on how many spatial coordinates its quantities genuinely depend on — a modelling choice that trades fidelity for cost.

Laminar versus turbulent

A laminar flow moves in orderly layers that do not mix; a turbulent flow is chaotic and three-dimensional, mixing momentum aggressively across those layers. Nearly every flow of interest here is turbulent, and the consequences run through everything downstream — drag, separation, heat transfer, and the entire structure of a CFD turbulence model.

The Reynolds number

The parameter that decides which regime prevails is the Reynolds number, the ratio of inertial to viscous forces:

Re = ρ U L / µ = U L / ν

Below a critical value viscosity damps disturbances and the flow stays laminar; above it, inertia wins and disturbances grow into turbulence. Re is the single most important dimensionless group in this whole subject: it sets the flow regime, it is the group that must be matched for a scale model to be representative, and it appears again and again in the chapters that follow. Its central role in wind-tunnel scaling is taken up in Part IV.

Circulation

Circulation Γ is the line integral of velocity around a closed loop — a scalar measure of the net rotation the loop encloses. It reads as an abstract definition on first encounter, but it is the quantity that ties directly to lift through the Kutta–Joukowski relation, and it earns its keep in Part II when lift generation is explained properly.
