The conservation laws

Foundations

All of fluid mechanics rests on three statements of conservation — of mass, momentum and energy — applied to a chosen region of fluid. Everything else is these three laws specialised to particular circumstances. It is worth meeting them first in their integral, control-volume form, because that is the form in which they are most obviously just bookkeeping.

Conservation of mass

Mass is neither created nor destroyed, so for a fixed control volume the rate at which mass accumulates inside equals the net rate at which it flows in across the boundaries. For a steady, incompressible flow the accumulation term vanishes and the statement reduces to something immediately useful: whatever volume flow enters a stream tube must leave it. Narrow the tube and the fluid must speed up. This continuity result is behind the acceleration through any contraction — a Venturi, the gap under a front wing, the throat of a diffuser.

Conservation of momentum

Newton's second law for a fluid: the net force on the fluid in a control volume equals the rate of change of its momentum plus the net momentum carried out across the boundaries. Its power is that it needs nothing about the messy internal details — knowing only the momentum crossing the inlet and outlet is enough to deduce the net force. This is precisely the argument used in Part II to show that a wing shedding a downward-deflected wake must feel an upward reaction, and it is the honest basis of the Newtonian view of lift.

Conservation of energy

Energy is conserved once every channel for it is accounted for — pressure work, kinetic energy, potential energy, heat, and any work done on or by the fluid. In the frictionless, incompressible limit this collapses to the balance between pressure and kinetic energy that is the subject of the next chapter.

Two ways of viewing the same laws recur throughout. The integral form tracks totals over a whole control volume and answers questions about net forces and flow rates. The differential form, applied at a point, becomes the Navier–Stokes equations that CFD solves. They are the same physics written at two scales.
