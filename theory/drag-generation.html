Drag generation

Theory · from TFM §2.1

Drag is the component of the aerodynamic force acting parallel to the freestream velocity. Recalling the force decomposition, it can be written as the sum of two integrals, and the split between them identifies the two physical origins of drag.

D = ∬A τw · eD dA − ∬A p n · eD dA

Skin friction drag

The first term corresponds to skin friction drag. It is produced by shear stress and it is always positive, meaning skin friction will always oppose resistance to motion through a fluid.

It is a consequence of viscous effects within the boundary layer. Normally the boundary layer begins in a laminar regime and, after a certain distance from the leading edge, transitions to turbulent; the total skin friction is the sum of both. The empirical friction coefficients are:

Laminar: Cf = 1.328 / √Rec · Turbulent: Cf = 0.074 / Rec1/5

This type of drag is obviously highly dependent on viscosity: a quick look at the expressions above gives the realization that higher viscosity results in a lower Reynolds number and therefore a higher friction coefficient. Skin friction is dominant in streamlined bodies with small thickness-to-length ratios (t/c ≪ 1). In motorsport, that is hardly the case.

Pressure drag

The second term is pressure drag, the integral of all normal stresses along the direction of the flux. It is caused by pressure imbalances and most often points backwards. It is the dominant type of drag in motorsport, where cars have intricate shapes producing complex pressure distributions. It can be sorted into form drag and induced drag.

Form drag is influenced directly by the shape of the body and the pressure distribution it causes, including separation and inertial effects. It is always present in a body in motion as long as there is a pressure imbalance, regardless of whether downforce is being generated — an infinite symmetrical airfoil at zero angle of attack still has form drag.

Induced drag exists only in wings that are both lift-generating and finite, and is treated separately in its own entry.

Wave drag is ignored here: it is caused by shock waves and is nonexistent at the subsonic regimes found in motorsport. Below M < 0.3 the flow is assumed incompressible.

Where the drag actually is, on a real car

In practice, the largest sources of drag on a race car narrow down to internal flow (radiators, HVAC, intake manifold), separated flow, or induced drag — the cost of generating downforce. All of these dependencies are characterized within the drag coefficient CD = D / (½ρU²S), and drag grows quadratically with velocity: doubling the speed gives four times the drag.
