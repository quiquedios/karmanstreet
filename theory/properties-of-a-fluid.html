Properties of a fluid

Foundations

A fluid is any substance that cannot resist a shear stress at rest — it deforms continuously for as long as the stress is applied, however small. That single property is what separates a fluid from a solid and is the reason the whole subject exists. Before any flow can be described, the properties that characterise the fluid itself need to be pinned down.

Density and compressibility

Density ρ is mass per unit volume. Whether it can be treated as constant is the first question of any analysis. Liquids are conventionally called incompressible and gases compressible, but the honest statement is that the distinction depends on the flow, not only on the fluid: a gas behaves incompressibly as long as its speed is small compared with the speed of sound. In motorsport the flow stays below roughly M < 0.3, so air is treated as incompressible throughout — the same assumption made everywhere in these notes unless stated otherwise.

Viscosity

Viscosity is the fluid's resistance to shear. It is easiest to see in the idealised case of a fluid filling the gap between two parallel plates, the lower one fixed and the upper one dragged along at a steady speed. The fluid ends up with a linear velocity profile across the gap, and the shear stress needed to sustain the motion is proportional to the velocity gradient:

τ = µ du/dy

This is Newton's law of viscosity, and fluids that obey it — air and water among them — are called Newtonian. The constant µ is the dynamic viscosity; dividing by density gives the kinematic viscosity ν = µ/ρ, which is the form that appears in the Reynolds number.

Temperature moves viscosity in opposite directions for gases and liquids, and the reason is physical. In a gas, viscosity comes from molecules of different layers mixing; heating them makes that thermal motion more vigorous, so mixing increases and viscosity rises. In a liquid, viscosity comes from intermolecular attraction; heating separates the molecules and weakens it, so viscosity falls. It is a useful sanity check to remember which way each goes.

A fluid idealised as having no viscosity at all is called an ideal or perfect fluid. It is a fiction, but a productive one — potential flow theory is built on it — and knowing exactly which real effect has been thrown away is what lets you judge where the idealisation will fail.

The perfect gas

For the air we deal with, the equation of state p = ρRT ties pressure, density and temperature together through the specific gas constant R. It is what justifies treating density as fixed once temperature and pressure are, and it underlies the standard-atmosphere values used to set up any simulation or tunnel test.
