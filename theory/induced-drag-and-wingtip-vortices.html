Induced drag and wingtip vortices

Theory · sources: thesis · E · G

Induced drag exists only in wings that are simultaneously (a) lift or downforce generating and (b) finite. Both conditions are necessary, and both are met by every wing on a race car.

The out-of-plane effect

Acknowledging the natural tendency of air to move from regions of high pressure to areas of low pressure, the air on the pressure side is driven around the tip to the suction side. This three-dimensional, out-of-plane effect is only noticeable at the tip of finite wings, and is most significant in those with low aspect ratios — which, again, describes motorsport wings.

The wingtip vortices generated in these cases have two effects:

  • They modify the pressure distribution, generating an imbalance in net pressure.
  • They extract kinetic energy from the flow, increasing air resistance.

Why endplates exist

Since induced drag is the price paid for downforce on a finite, low-aspect-ratio wing, the obvious countermeasure is to interfere with the tip roll-up. That is exactly what endplates do: a physical barrier keeping the airflow on the pressure side isolated from the suction side.

This is the last of the four steps in the basic recipe for a motorsport wing — inverted profile, close to the ground, multi-element, and endplated — and the only one aimed specifically at drag rather than at downforce.
