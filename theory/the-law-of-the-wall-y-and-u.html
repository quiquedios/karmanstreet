The law of the wall; y+ and u+

Theory · sources: B · thesis

Independently of the turbulence model, a decision must be made on how the near-wall region is represented on the mesh, since it is there that the steepest velocity gradients of the entire flow occur.

The law of the wall

The law of the wall describes the near-wall structure of a turbulent boundary layer. Such a layer is conventionally divided, near the wall, into three regions:

  1. The viscous sublayer, immediately adjacent to the wall, where molecular viscosity dominates and the velocity grows almost linearly from zero.
  2. The buffer layer, an intermediate zone in which neither viscous nor turbulent stresses are negligible.
  3. The fully turbulent logarithmic layer, where the eddy viscosity dominates and the velocity follows the logarithmic law.

Capturing the momentum exchange across these regions is what ultimately determines the predicted wall shear stress and, with it, the onset of separation.

The dimensionless variables

The structure is made quantitative through two dimensionless quantities, the wall-normal coordinate y+ and the velocity u+ = u/uτ:

y⁺ = uτ y / ν , uτ = √(τw/ρ)

where y is the physical wall-normal distance of the first cell centroid, ν the kinematic viscosity, τw the wall shear stress and uτ the friction velocity built from it. Because y+ scales the distance by the local flow conditions at the wall, the same physical first-cell height corresponds to different y+ values depending on the speed and the state of the boundary layer. It is y+, and not the raw distance, that must be controlled when making meshing decisions.

Plotting u+ against y+ on a semi-logarithmic scale yields a universal velocity profile, remarkably consistent across wall-bounded turbulent flows, in which the three regions become apparent. In the viscous sublayer the relation is linear, u+ = y+, whereas in the logarithmic layer it follows u+ = (1/κ) ln y+ + B, with κ ≈ 0.41 the von Kármán constant and B ≈ 5.0 for smooth walls. These two expressions are asymptotes joined by the buffer layer, in which neither holds exactly.

Two strategies, and the gap between them

Two approaches exist to represent this structure on a mesh, and each is valid only in the region whose profile it assumes.

The wall-resolved approach refines the mesh sufficiently to place several cells inside the viscous sublayer and integrate the equations directly to the surface; the first cell must lie within the sublayer, at a target of y+ ≈ 1 and in any case below y+ ≈ 5. It is the most accurate route, but the near-wall mesh it demands is extremely dense and raises the cell count, and therefore the memory expenditure, dramatically.

The wall-function approach does the opposite: it does not resolve the sublayer at all, but places the first cell within the logarithmic layer (conventionally 30 ≲ y+ ≲ 300) and bridges the region between it and the wall with the analytical law-of-the-wall profile. It is substantially cheaper, at the cost of relying on the equilibrium assumptions embedded in that profile, which lose validity under strong adverse pressure gradients or separated flow.

The intervening buffer layer (5 ≲ y+ ≲ 30) is to be avoided for the first cell under either approach, as it is precisely where neither asymptote is accurate.

In the thesis work the near-wall region was treated with wall functions. It must be stressed that this was not the preferred choice on accuracy grounds but a constraint imposed by the computational resources available: the memory limit of the platform used does not permit the dense near-wall mesh a wall-resolved treatment of a full multi-element front wing would require while still reaching mesh-size independence. The residual loss of near-wall fidelity is accepted as a conscious trade-off.
